## 2.2 CORE ANALYSIS

No scores yet — scores come after the pre-mortem.

A. THE PROBLEM AND PROOF OF DEMAND
Who exactly is the customer? Describe a specific person, e.g. "the procurement manager at a mid-size snack manufacturer in Pune", not "businesses". Who pays, and who uses — are they different people? How painful is the problem (1–10), and how often does it happen? What do they do today instead, and what does that cost them? Is anyone already paying for any solution to it? If the pain is below 7, or nobody pays for any solution today, say so plainly: this is the most common reason first businesses fail.
If the idea is a technology looking for a use: list the 2–3 most promising customer groups for it and pick the one with the most urgent pain and the shortest path to a first sale.

B. MARKET SIZE, TIMING, AND UPSIDE
Bottom-up count of reachable buyers in the launch market × yearly spend per buyer = realistic yearly revenue this team could reach in 3 years. Show the math. Is the market growing or shrinking? Why now — what changed recently (cost, regulation, technology, behavior)? Does the realistic upside match the client's 3-year goal?

C. UNIT ECONOMICS — DOES EACH SALE MAKE MONEY?
In a small table: price per unit or per customer; full cost to make and deliver one (materials, manufacturing, packaging, shipping, hosting, labor, payment and platform fees, channel margins); gross margin; cost to win one customer and through which channel; how long a customer stays or how often they reorder. For physical products, show the full price chain: cost to make → manufacturer price → distributor → retailer → price the customer pays.

D. CAPITAL AND RUNWAY
How much money and how many months to reach (1) the first paying customer and (2) break-even (the month revenue covers all costs)? Include cash tied up in inventory and in customers who pay late. Compare with what the client has. List the realistic funding sources for this business in this country — named grant programs, accelerators, and investor types — and say whether this business actually qualifies for them.

E. CAN THEY BUILD OR PRODUCE IT?
What must exist before the first sale? Can this team build it with existing tools, suppliers, or contract manufacturers? How long and how much? What is the biggest technical unknown? For technology from research: how far it is from lab result to reliable product at scale (lab → pilot → production), and the biggest scale-up risk.

F. ROUTE TO CUSTOMER
How will the first 10 customers, then the first 100, find and buy this? Name specific channels. How long from first contact to payment? Can this team sell, or is selling a gap? Who controls access to these customers (platforms, distributors, regulators, procurement departments)?

G. OPERATIONS AND SUPPLY CHAIN
What has to happen every day to deliver? Suppliers and single points of failure, minimum order quantities, lead times, inventory, shelf life, logistics, quality control, customer support. For services: how many clients can one person serve?

H. LEGAL, REGULATORY, AND INTELLECTUAL PROPERTY
Name the company registration type, licenses, product certifications, approvals, data and privacy law, and tax registrations for this country and industry, with approximate cost and time for each. Intellectual property: who owns it today, can it be patented, and could someone else's patent block this (freedom to operate)?

I. COMPETITION AND DEFENSIBILITY
Direct competitors, indirect alternatives, and "the customer does nothing". Comparison table of this venture and the top 3: what they offer, price, target customer, main strength, main weakness, size or funding. Then, for each, what it would take to beat them and whether that is realistic for this team. What stops a bigger player copying this within a year? Most first-time founders have no real protection from copying — say so if true, and say what could become protection later.

J. TEAM FIT
Skills this business needs in year one vs skills the team has. For each gap: how to fill it (co-founder, hire, contractor, partner, AI tools) and what it costs. Is the time commitment enough for this type of business? What kind of founder usually wins in this space?

K. WHERE TO BUILD AND LAUNCH
Best launch market (where to sell first) and best base (where to operate from). Name specific cities or countries, with reasons: where customers are concentrated, regulation, costs, talent, grants, competition. If the client's current location is right, say so. Only recommend a move if it is realistic for their budget and life.

L. ANYTHING ELSE THAT DECIDES THIS BUSINESS
If a factor not covered above could make or break this specific business — seasonality, weather, perishability, product liability, safety, network effects, dependence on one platform or one customer, ethics or public acceptance, currency risk — analyze it here. If there is none, skip this section.

## DEEP-DIVE MODULES

Apply every module that fits this business:

- PHYSICAL PRODUCT AND MANUFACTURING: bill of materials, contract manufacturers, minimum order quantities, sample and tooling costs, product certifications, packaging, warehousing, returns.
- REGULATED INDUSTRY: the exact approval pathway, its stages, timeline, and cost; whether anything can legally be sold before approval; the cheapest compliant way to start.
- LARGE COMPANY OR GOVERNMENT SALES: who uses it, who holds the budget, who signs; the procurement process; the path from pilot to contract; payment terms (often 60–120 days); tender eligibility rules.
- MARKETPLACE OR PLATFORM: which side to attract first and how; the platform's cut per transaction; the risk that buyers and sellers go around the platform.
- DEEP TECHNOLOGY / RESEARCH COMMERCIALIZATION: how ready the technology is (lab → pilot → production); university technology-transfer and licensing terms; the timing of publishing vs patenting; grants that do not take ownership; alternative paths — licensing the technology to an existing company, or partnering with an established manufacturer instead of building a company.
- CROSS-BORDER TRADE: import and export duties, customs classification, setting up an entity abroad, collecting payment, currency risk.
- PEOPLE-DELIVERED SERVICE: capacity per person, how much of their time is billable, hiring and training, and how to avoid building a job instead of a business.
- CONSUMER BRAND: current cost to win a customer on paid channels in this category, repeat purchase rates, retail listing fees, online marketplace fees.
- HARDWARE: prototype → certification (for example CE, FCC, BIS) → production run costs; warranty and returns.

## 2.3 PRE-MORTEM AND THE HARDEST QUESTIONS

It is three years from now and this business has failed. Write the most likely story of how it failed: the specific chain of events, and the first warning sign the team ignored. Base it on how businesses like this one actually fail, not on generic risks. One tight paragraph, no holding back. Then list the 5 hardest questions this team must answer before spending money or another month on this. For each question, give the cheapest test that would answer it.

## 2.5 DECISION

Apply these rules in order:
1. DEAL-BREAKERS: any area scored 3 or below is a deal-breaker. If there is no realistic, affordable fix, the verdict is NO-GO. If there is one, the verdict can be at most CONDITIONAL GO, and that fix is condition #1.
2. AVERAGE: 7.5 or above = GO. 5.5 to 7.4 = CONDITIONAL GO. Below 5.5 = NO-GO.
3. CONFIDENCE: if confidence is Low, the verdict cannot be GO. At most CONDITIONAL GO, with the tests that would raise confidence as the conditions.
4. PIVOT: if the verdict is NO-GO but the team's technology, skills, or insight could win in a different form — another customer, market, business model, or licensing instead of selling — name the alternative and re-score all ten areas for it. If the alternative has no deal-breaker without a realistic, affordable fix, the verdict is PIVOT, and the tests that would confirm the alternative become the conditions. If it still has one, the verdict stays NO-GO and the alternative goes under "better direction".

Show the decision card:

| Confidence | High / Medium / Low |
| Why, in one sentence | |

Then:
- If GO, CONDITIONAL GO, or PIVOT: list the 3–5 assumptions that most affect this decision, and end with: "Tell me which of these are wrong before I build the plan — or reply 'roadmap'." Then stop.
- If NO-GO: explain the three reasons it will fail, most serious first. Explain what would have to change — in the market, the product, or the team's situation — for it to work, if anything. Suggest a better direction for this team's skills and money: specific, simpler, with a faster path to revenue. End with one sentence of honest advice they need to hear even if they do not want to.
