# The twelve lenses

Each lens is a *source of change*. You are not looking for problems — problems are permanent and
mostly unsolvable. You are looking for problems that **recently became solvable, or recently
became urgent**, because that is the only reason a one-person company can beat an incumbent.

For each lens: what it is, where to look, what a real find looks like, and how it goes wrong.

---

## 1. Cost collapse
An input fell more than half in price within about three years, so something uneconomic is now
economic.
**Search:** component and commodity price indices, teardown reports, cloud and API price change
logs, freight rates, equipment resale listings.
**A real find:** a named input, its price then and now, and one thing that was not worth doing at
the old price.
**Fails when:** you cite the trend without the buyer. Cheap sensors are not a business; a farmer
who currently pays someone to walk the field is.

## 2. New rule with a date
A law, standard or mandate that forces a class of organisations to do something by a deadline.
The deadline is the whole opportunity: it converts a nice-to-have into a budget line.
**Search:** government gazettes, regulator sites, compliance-consultancy blogs, trade-body
newsletters. Search the phase-in schedule, not the headline.
**A real find:** the rule, the date, who it binds, and what they must produce to comply.
**Fails when:** the rule is old news and a compliance industry already exists. Check who is
already selling to it before you call it an opening.

## 3. Capability commoditized
Work that needed a trained specialist now needs a tool and an afternoon.
**Search:** what junior roles are disappearing from job boards; what agencies quietly stopped
charging for; what became a checkbox inside a larger product.
**A real find:** the task, who used to be paid for it, what they charged, and who still pays that
price because they do not know it changed.
**Fails when:** it becomes "AI does X". The opening is a *specific priced task*, not a capability.

## 4. Demographic turn
A population curve crosses a threshold: a cohort ages into a need, household sizes shift, a
workforce retires, migration concentrates demand somewhere new.
**Search:** census releases, labour statistics, pension and health system projections.
**A real find:** a cohort, its size, the year it crosses the threshold, and what it will need that
nobody is currently staffed to supply.
**Fails when:** you describe a megatrend. "Aging population" is not a find. "Roughly N thousand
skilled tool-and-die operators in this district retire within four years and the apprenticeship
pipeline produces M a year" is.

## 5. Behaviour outran the tooling
People are already doing serious, valuable work with duct tape — spreadsheets, WhatsApp groups,
shared folders, a part-time person. They have already proved the demand. Nobody has productised it.
**Search:** job ads describing the manual process, template marketplaces, forum threads sharing
spreadsheets, "how we do X" operational blog posts.
**A real find:** the workaround, how many people use it, and the hours or errors it costs.
**Fails when:** the duct tape is genuinely good enough. Some spreadsheets should stay spreadsheets.

## 6. Unbundling
One feature of a large product is one person's entire job, and they are paying for the other
ninety features to get it.
**Search:** review sites filtered to complaints about price and complexity; "we only use it for"
phrasing; feature-request boards that go unanswered for years.
**A real find:** the suite, its price, the single feature, and a role whose whole day is that feature.
**Fails when:** the incumbent can switch it off by shipping the feature properly. Ask what stops them.

## 7. Platform opening
A new distribution surface exists and supply has not caught up: a new marketplace, an app
ecosystem, an assistant or agent directory, a payments rail, a government portal with an API.
**Search:** platform changelogs and developer announcements, new-category launches, directories
sorted by newest.
**A real find:** the surface, when it opened, how buyers currently discover things on it, and how
thin supply is.
**Fails when:** you are building on a platform that can delete you. Say who controls it and what
happens if they change the rules.

## 8. Abandoned segment
An incumbent left customers stranded: moved upmarket, raised prices, discontinued a product,
was acquired and gutted, or exited a country.
**Search:** end-of-life and sunset notices, price-increase announcements, acquisition news
followed by review-site anger, "alternatives to X" traffic.
**A real find:** the vendor, what they abandoned, when, and where those customers went.
**Fails when:** they were abandoned because serving them lost money. Work out why the incumbent
left before assuming you can stay.

## 9. Supply relocation
Production, sourcing or routing is moving — tariffs, reshoring, a new corridor, a port or
plant opening, an export restriction.
**Search:** trade notifications, tariff schedules, industrial park and plant announcements,
logistics trade press.
**A real find:** what moved, from where to where, by when, and which intermediary that breaks.
**Fails when:** you need capital and containers. Look for the service and information layers
around the move, which is where one person can work.

## 10. Resource constraint
Water, power, heat, land, or a specific skilled trade is short somewhere specific, and the
shortage has a date and a place.
**Search:** utility and grid reports, drought and heat records, industrial-permit refusals,
wage data for trades with vacancies.
**A real find:** the resource, the place, the shortage, and who is already paying a premium.
**Fails when:** it is a global abstraction. Constraints are always local; the business is local too.

## 11. Public complaint data
Thousands of people describing the same failure in public. It is the cheapest demand evidence
that exists, and almost nobody reads it systematically.
**Search:** one-star reviews on app stores and marketplaces sorted by recency, support forums,
trade subreddits, consumer complaint registries, refund policy threads.
**A real find:** the same specific complaint repeated by a countable number of people, with what
they tried instead.
**Fails when:** you find complaints about price. Everyone complains about price; it tells you nothing.

## 12. Infrastructure just finished
Something large got built and the things that should run on top of it have not been built: a
payments rail, a digital identity system, a logistics network, a data standard, a grid connection.
**Search:** announcements of go-live, adoption statistics a year later, the gap between
"available" and "used".
**A real find:** the infrastructure, when it went live, its adoption today, and what is missing
above it.
**Fails when:** adoption is near zero for a reason. Find out whether anyone actually wants to use it.

## 13. Exists elsewhere, not here
A product, service or business model that is ordinary in another country and absent, or
present only in a bad form, in India. The oldest way to start a company, and the most
commonly botched.

**Search:** category leaders in the US, UK, China, Brazil, Indonesia and the Gulf, then
check for an Indian equivalent. Look at what Y Combinator funded three years ago and what
of it has no Indian counterpart. Read foreign trade press for categories you have never
heard of. Compare "top 20 apps" lists across countries.

**A real find:** the thing, where it works, how long it has worked there, what it costs
there, and evidence that no Indian version exists or that the existing ones are bad.

**The question that separates a real find from a bad one:** *what structural precondition
made this work over there, and does India have it yet?* Something works in the US because
labour is expensive, addresses are standardised, card penetration is universal, and
customers will pay a subscription. Some of those now hold in India and some never will.
Name the precondition explicitly and say whether it is present, arriving, or absent.

- **Absent:** a service that replaces cheap manual labour is worth far less where labour
  is cheap. Do not transplant it.
- **Arriving:** the interesting case. UPI made small-ticket payments free, ONDC changed
  discovery, cheap data changed who is online, GST created a digital paper trail for
  millions of small firms. Something that failed in India five years ago may work now for
  a reason you can name and date.
- **Present:** then ask why nobody has done it. Usually someone tried. Find them.

**Fails when:** you list a foreign company and add "for India". That is not an idea, it is
a mapping. The idea is the specific Indian buyer, the specific precondition that recently
became true, and what has to be different here — usually the price point, the channel, and
how much hand-holding the first hundred customers need.

**Also run it in reverse.** Things India does that the world does not: UPI-scale real-time
payments, dabbawala-grade physical routing, jugaad repair economies, an enormous multilingual
informal sector. Occasionally the export is the opportunity, and an Indian founder is the
natural person to build it.
