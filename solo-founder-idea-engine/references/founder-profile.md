## What this founder can actually leverage

Be concrete about the advantages, because most ideas fail the fit test rather than the
market test.

**Time against money.** They can spend three weeks doing something manual and unscalable
that a funded team would never authorise. Ideas whose first version is the founder doing
the work by hand are a *fit*, not a compromise. Ideas needing inventory, deposits, or six
months of runway before first revenue are not.

**Mumbai's density.** Almost nowhere else in India lets one person meet this many businesses
on foot in a week. Any idea whose validation needs thirty conversations is cheap here and
expensive elsewhere. Lean on that: prefer ideas where the buyer is physically in this city.

**What Mumbai concentrates**, and most of it is under-served by software: BFSI and the
back-office around it, logistics and the port, wholesale and distribution markets, textiles
and apparel, diamonds and jewellery, film, advertising and media production, pharma
distribution, real estate and construction, hospitality, and an enormous density of small
traders and family-run firms running on paper, WhatsApp and Tally.

**Being in their 20s** means they can do unglamorous, low-status legwork that older founders
avoid — standing in a wholesale market, riding along on deliveries, sitting in a clinic
waiting room — and that is where the observations worth building on actually come from.

## What this founder cannot leverage

Say so plainly when an idea needs one of these.

- **Credibility with large buyers.** No track record. Enterprise and government procurement
  will stall for a year. Prefer buyers who can decide alone: owner-operators, small firms,
  individual professionals.
- **Capital.** No inventory-first business, no tooling, no deposits, no paid acquisition as
  the primary channel.
- **Industry relationships.** They cannot get a meeting on a phone call. Any idea assuming
  warm access to an industry is assuming something they do not have.
- **Waiting.** Limited money means revenue has to start in weeks, not quarters. Regulated
  paths needing an approval before any sale are a poor fit unless something can be sold
  legally while the approval is pending.

## The reachability rule

| If the idea is | Then the market must be |
|---|---|
| Software, SaaS, an API, content, a remote service | Anywhere, provided the buyer can pay across borders and the founder can support their timezone |
| A service needing site visits, inspection, or physical presence | Mumbai metro |
| A physical product | India first, unless a named export route already works |
| Anything regulated | A jurisdiction the founder can register and operate in — realistically India |

**This is the test that fails most often.** A compliance tool for a US federal rule is
reachable: it is software, and US firms buy software from Indian vendors every day. A
thermal-scanning service for contractors in a US metro is not, because someone has to hold
the camera.

At least **3 of 5 ideas each run must be reachable in person**, not merely sellable remotely.

## Where the search skews

Web search returns disproportionately US sources, so US regulations, US vendors and US
complaint data surface first. Left unchecked every run drifts to American small business.
Counter it deliberately:

- Search Indian sources by name: the Gazette of India, CPCB, FSSAI, SEBI, RBI, IRDAI,
  MeitY, TRAI, DGFT, state pollution control boards, GST notifications, DPIIT, MSME schemes,
  BMC and MMRDA for Mumbai specifically.
- When a change is found abroad, check whether India has a counterpart and when it lands.
- Read Indian trade press, not just global tech press.
