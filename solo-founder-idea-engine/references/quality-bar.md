# What makes an idea good, and what makes a strategy good

Two different questions. A strong idea with no strategy is a wish. A sharp strategy
pointed at a weak idea is wasted effort. Score both, and rank the run.

---

## Part one — the idea

Eight dimensions. Score each 1–5 and show the total out of 40. These are not arbitrary:
each one is a way solo businesses actually die.

### 1. Urgency, not preference
Is the buyer bleeding, or would they merely like this?

> Deadline-driven (a date forces it) **5** · Bleeding (it costs them money now) **4**
> · Painful (annoying, tolerated) **2** · Preference (nice to have) **1**

A compliance date beats a good product. Someone with a deadline buys from whoever shows up.

### 2. Existing budget line
Does money already flow for this in some form, or would someone have to start spending on
a new category?

Redirecting an existing spend is a sale. Creating a new spend is education, and education
is a marketing budget a solo founder does not have. **This is the single strongest predictor
of whether one person can sell it.**

### 3. Frequency
How often does the pain occur? Daily is bought quickly; annual is tolerated and forgotten.
Annual pain also means an annual sales cycle, which starves a founder with limited capital.

### 4. Concentration
Can you reach a hundred of these buyers in one place — one market, one association, one
platform, one Mumbai neighbourhood, one WhatsApp group?

Diffuse buyers kill solo founders more reliably than bad products. If the answer is "they
are all over India", score it 1 and look for the subset that clusters.

### 5. Margin after everyone's cut
What is left once manufacturing, channel and payment fees take theirs? Enough to pay for
acquisition, or is the only viable channel a free one? Thin margin is survivable *only*
with a free channel; thin margin plus paid acquisition is a machine for losing money.

### 6. Repeat
Do they come back, or is every rupee a fresh hunt? Subscription, consumable, seasonal
reorder, or retainer all beat one-off. One-off is not disqualifying, but it means the
business is a treadmill, and that should be said out loud.

### 7. Timing window
Why now, and **when does this close**? The change should be recent enough that incumbents
have not responded, and you should be able to say what ends the window — the incumbent
ships it, the rule matures, a funded competitor arrives. An idea with no closing date was
probably always possible, which means someone already tried.

### 8. Falsifiable this week
Can one cheap check kill it in days? An idea you cannot disprove quickly will consume
months before it tells you anything.

**Rank the five ideas by total. Say which is strongest and why.** A run that presents five
ideas as equals has not done the work.

## Part two — the strategy

An idea without these is not executable, however good the opportunity.

### One wedge, not a platform
The narrowest thing a specific buyer will pay for **now**. Platforms are what wedges grow
into; they are never where you start. If the description needs the word "and" twice, it is
too wide.

### One channel, named, with a reason
Not a list. The question is never "what channels exist" — it is "where are these hundred
buyers already gathered, and what does it cost to stand there?" Founders who name five
channels use none of them properly.

### A first-ten plan made of real names
Not a persona. An actual list you could build this week: this market, that association's
member directory, these ten shops on this road. If you cannot describe how to build the
list, the strategy is decoration.

### Pricing shaped like how they already buy
Match the existing habit rather than teaching a new one. Many Indian small firms pay per
project or annually and are wary of monthly auto-debits; institutions need an invoice and
pay in 60–120 days. Pricing that fights the buyer's habit loses sales that the product
had already won.

### A reason they tell someone
Within a trade, in a dense city, word of mouth is the cheapest channel there is — but only
when the result is visible and worth mentioning. What exactly would the first customer say,
and to whom?

### An explicit "not yet" list
What you deliberately will not build, sell or chase in the first six months. Strategy is
mostly subtraction, and a founder with limited capital who skips this will do month-nine
work in month one.

### Sequencing
What must be true before the next step. If step two depends on something step one does not
produce, the plan is a wish list rather than a sequence.

## Demand fundamentals — ask these before believing any of it

- **Painkiller or vitamin?** In a tight month, what gets cancelled first? If this does, it
  is a vitamin, and vitamins need a marketing budget.
- **Who pays, who uses, who decides?** When they differ, the strategy is aimed at whoever
  signs, and the product at whoever uses.
- **What job is it hired for** — functionally, and emotionally? D2C especially is bought
  for what it says about the buyer as much as what it does.
- **What do they do today?** If the honest answer is "nothing, and they are fine", there is
  no business, regardless of how real the problem looks on paper.
